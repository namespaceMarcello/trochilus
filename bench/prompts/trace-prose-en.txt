Hummingbirds are the smallest birds in the world, and almost everything about them follows from the way they fly. A hummingbird does not glide and does not soar. It hovers, which means that it holds its body still in the air while its wings sweep forward and backward in a flat figure of eight, fifty or sixty times every second in the smaller species. Most birds produce lift only on the downstroke. A hummingbird turns its wing over at the end of each half stroke, so that the backstroke lifts as well, a little less than the forward one. The motion is closer to that of a large moth than to that of a sparrow, and people who see a hawk moth at dusk for the first time often take it for a very small bird.

Hovering is expensive. Weight for weight, a hovering hummingbird burns energy faster than any other warm blooded animal that has been measured. Its heart can beat more than a thousand times a minute, and it breathes several times a second even at rest. To pay for this it drinks nectar, which is little more than sugar dissolved in water, and it drinks a great deal of it: in a day a bird may take in more than its own weight. Sugar alone is not enough to live on, so it also catches small insects and spiders, which give it the protein and the fat that nectar lacks. A large part of its day is spent sitting still on a twig, digesting, waiting for the crop to empty so that it can feed again.

The night is the hard part. A bird this small loses heat quickly, it cannot feed in the dark, and it carries almost no reserve. Many species answer the problem with torpor. When the evening is cold and the day has been poor, the bird lets its body temperature fall by twenty degrees or more, its heart slows to a few dozen beats a minute, and its use of energy drops to a small fraction of the daytime rate. In the morning it shivers itself warm again, which takes about twenty minutes and costs energy of its own, and then it goes looking for the first flowers.

Flowers and hummingbirds have shaped each other. Plants that depend on these birds tend to have red or orange tubular flowers, with no scent, no place for an insect to land, and nectar set deep at the bottom of the tube. The bird reaches it with a long bill and a longer tongue, forked at the tip and fringed along the edges, which traps the liquid and pulls it back into the mouth many times a second. While it drinks, pollen is dusted on its forehead or its throat, and it is carried to the next flower of the same kind.
